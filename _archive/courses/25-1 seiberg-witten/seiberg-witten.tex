\subsection*{Introduction}

SW theory $\subset$ Gauge theory.
We do not regard physical aspects.

Gauge theory: Using some non-linear PDE, study the topology of smooth 4-manifolds.

Two big methods:
\begin{enumerate}
\item 
By ``counting'' solutions to the PDE, construct an invariant of smooth 4-manifolds.
Distinguish smooth 4-manifolds (detect exotic 4-manifolds, and two smooth manifolds are called \emph{exotic} if they are homeomorphic but not diffeomorphic)
\item
By studying the ``solution space''(moduli space) for the PDE, give a constraint on the topology of smooth 4-manifolds. (Donaldson)
Detect non-smoothable topological 4-manifolds, such manifolds are known to exist for dimension $\ge4$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Intersection form}

Let $X$ be an oriented closed topological 4-manifold.
Then, $Q_X:(H^2(X,\Z)/\Tor)^{\otimes2}\to\Z:\alpha\otimes\beta\mapsto\<\alpha\smile\beta,[X]\>$ is called the \emph{intersection form}, and it is clearly homotopy invariant.
As a remark, by the Poincar\'e duality we can identify $H^2(X,\Z)$ with $H_2(X,\Z)$, and $Q_X(\alpha,\beta)$ means $\#(\Sigma\cap\Sigma')$ counting with sign, where $\alpha=[\Sigma]$ and $\beta=[\Sigma']$ with $\Sigma$ and $\Sigma'$ oriented closed embedded surfaces in $X$ (existence of such representative may not be clear for topological cases, but relatively clear for smooth cases.).

For example if $X=S^4$, then $H^2(S^4)=0$ and $Q_{S^4}=*$ with size zero.

For example if $X=\CP^2$, then $H_2(\CP^2)=\Z[\CP^1]$ and $Q_{\CP^2}=(1)$ with size one.

For example if $X=S^2\times S^2$, then $H_2(S^2\times S^2)=\Z[S^2\times*]\oplus\Z[*\times S^2]$ and $Q_{S^2\times S^2}=\left(\mat[small]{0&1\\1&0}\right)$.
We can check self-intersection vanishes and off-diagonal part has a single intersection. (perturbing using trivial normal bundle?)

In general, $Q_X$ is symmetric.
If we consider the same device for 2-dimensional, it becomes anti-symmetric.
It is also bilinear over $\Z$ and non-degenerate.

\begin{exercise}
The intersection form is non-degenerate.
Use the Poincar\'e duality.
\end{exercise}

There is a converse question.
When is a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form over $\Z$ the intersection form of a topological 4-manifold?

\begin{theorem}[Freedman, 1982]
Let $Q$ be a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form over $\Z$.
Then, there is a topological simply connected closed orientable 4-manifold $X$ such that $Q_X\cong Q$ over $\Z$.
Moreover, such $X$ are at most two up to homeomorphism, and determined by the Kirby-Siebenmann invariant $KS(X)\in H^4(X,\Z/2\Z)\cong\Z/2\Z$.
\end{theorem}

(proved by wild topology such as approximation with Cantor sets, and there would be almost no people understanding the proof.)

This theorem says that the intersection form is strong enough in topological cases.
Also it is known that the Kirby-Siebenmann invariant of a smoothable simply connected closed orientable 4-manifold vanishes.

In general, there are many definite quadratic form $Q$, i.e.~$Q(\alpha,\alpha)\ge0$ for all $\alpha$ or $Q(\alpha,\alpha)\ge0$ for all $\alpha$, when fixed the rank.
If the rank is $32$, then there are many more than $10^7$.


\begin{theorem}[Donaldson's diagonalization theorem, 1983]
If $X$ is a smooth closed orientable 4-manifold (originally assumed to be simply connected, but it is revealed that it is not required) with $Q_X$ is negative-definite, then $Q_X$ is isomorphic to $\diag(-1,\cdots,-1)$ over $\Z$.
\end{theorem}

In particular, there should be many many non-smoothable topological 4-manifolds by the diagonalization theorem and Freedman's thoeorem.


High-dimensional $>4$, exotic structures can be detected by characteristic classes, which is a dominating method.
Simply connected 4-manifolds, it is hard to say that there is another method to detect such phenomena...?

More about intersection forms:
\begin{definition}
Let $Q:L\otimes L\to\Z$ be a symmetric non-degenerate bilinear form over $\Z$, where $L$ is a lattice, i.e.~finitely generated free abelian group.
Then,
\[b^+(Q):=\text{max dim of positive-definite subspace of $L\otimes_\Z\R$ with respect to $Q$},\]
\[b^-(Q):=\text{max dim of negative-definite subspace of $L\otimes_\Z\R$ with respect to $Q$},\]
and the signature
\[\sigma(Q):=b^+(Q)-b^-(Q).\]
Note that the rank of $L$ is $b^+(Q)+b^-(Q)$.

Also, $Q$ is called \emph{even} if $Q(\alpha,\alpha)\cong0$ modulo $2$ for all $\alpha$, and \emph{odd} if it not even.

When $X$ is a closed orientable topological 4-manifold, then we use the following notations:
\[b^\pm(X):=b^\pm(Q_X),\qquad\sigma(X):=\sigma(Q_X),\]
and parity and definiteness of $X$ is defined by the parity and definiteness of $Q_X$.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
\[\begin{array}{c|cccc}
&b^+&b^-&\sigma&\text{parity}\\\hline
S^4&0&0&0&\text{even}\\
\CP^2&1&0&1&\text{odd}\\
S^2\times S^2&1&1&0&\text{even}\\
K3&3&19&-16&\text{even}
\end{array}\]
\end{example}

\subsection{SW invariant}

Input of SW invariant: closed oriented smooth 4-manifold $X^4$ with $b^+(X)\ge2$.


\begin{remark}
There is a version for $b^+(X)=1$.
There is no reasonable definition of the SW invariant for $b^+(X)=0$.
\end{remark}

Output is a $\Z$-valued invariant $SW(X,\fs)\in\Z$, where $\fs$ is a spin$^c$ structure on $X$.
The sign of this invariant is determined by an orientation of $H^1(X,\R)\oplus^+(X)$ called a \emph{homology orientation}.
Here $H^+(X)$ is a maximal dimensional positive-definite subspace of $H^2(X,\R)$ with respect to $Q_X$, meaning that $Q_X|_{H^+(X)}$ is a positive definite and $\dim H^+(X)=b^+(X)$.


\begin{example}
Let $X=\CP^2\#2\bar\CP^2$.
Then, $H_2(X)=\Z H\oplus\Z E_1\oplus\Z E_2\cong\Z^3$, where $H$ is hyperplane class, and $E_1$ and $E_2$ are exceptional curve classes.
The smooth structure underlied on the connected sum with $\bar\CP^2$ corresponds to the blow-up at a point.
In $H_2(X,\R)$, the equation $Q_X\equiv0$ defines a subset of a real vector space $H_2(X,\R)$ which can be called the light cone, and $H^+(X)$ is a line in the `interior' of the light cone.
In this example, the set of choices of $H^+(X)$ is homeomorphic to the open unit disk.
\end{example}

In general, we have a following exercise, which is not easy to prove at a first glance.

\begin{lemma}
The moduli space of linear subspaces $H\subset H^2(X,\R)$ such that $b^+(X)=\dim H$ and $Q_X|_H$ is positive definite is contractible.
\end{lemma}

Thanks to this lemma, if you pick one choice of $H^+(X)$, and fix an orientation on it, then it induces an orientation on another choice of $H^+(X)$ in a canonical way.
Every choice of $H^+(X)$ is unique up to homotopy.

Let $\cO$ be a homology orientation on $X$.
Precisely, the SW invariant is an invariant of triple: $SW(X,\fd,\cO)\in\Z$ with property that $SW(X,\fs,-\cO)=-SW(X,\fs,\cO)$.
Usually $\cO$ is dropped from our notation.

We define the spin group $\Spin(n)$ as the unique double cover of $\SO(n)$ for $n\ge3$.

\begin{definition}
Let $X$ be a oriented smooth manifold of dimension $n$ equipped with a metric $g$.
A \emph{spin structure} $\fs$ on $X$ is a pair $(P,\Psi)$, where $P$ is a principal $\Spin(n)$-bundle over $X$ and $\Psi$ is an isomorphism of principal $\SO(n)$-bundles
\[\Psi:P\times_{\Spin(n)}\SO(n)\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathrm{Fr}(X).\]
We should not forget $\Psi$, a compatibility with the data of tangent bundle.
In other words, a spin structure is a choice of a lift of the frame bundle map $X\to B\SO(n)$ along $B\Spin(n)\to B\SO(n)$. Of course it is up to homotopy because classifying spaces are defined up to homotopy equivalence, which corresponds to isomorphism classes of principal $\Spin(n)$-bundles.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
To consider an isomorphism class of a spin($^c$) structure, one does not need to pick a metric, because $\GL^+(n,\R)$ is homotopy equivalent to $\SO(n)$.
However, a choice of a metric is needed to define the Dirac operator. 
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
A 4-manifold may or may not admit a spin structure.
For examples, $S^2\times S^2$ and $K3$ admit spin structures, but $\CP^2$ does not.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
\[\Spin^c(n):=\frac{\Spin(n)\times\rU(1)}{\Z/2\Z},\]
where $\Z/2\Z$ acts on $\Spin(n)$ as the covering transformation and on $\rU(1)$ faithfully as the scalar multiplication.
The alphabet $c$ stands for ``complex''.
We have a canonical surjection $\Spin^c(n)\to\SO(n)$.

\begin{definition}
Let $X$ be a oriented smooth $n$-manifold equipped with a metric.
A \emph{spin$^c$ structure} $\fs$ on $X$ is a pair $(P,\Psi)$, where $P$ is a principal $\Spin^c(n)$-bundle and $\Psi$ is an isomorphism of principal $\SO(n)$-bundles
\[\Psi:P\times_{\Spin^c(n)}\SO(n)\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathrm{Fr}(X).\]
\end{definition}

There is a fact that every oriented 4-manifold admits a spin$^c$ structure.

We usually do not use this fact because almost every argument begin with fixing a concretely given spin$^c$ structure.

\subsection{SW equations}
Let $X$ be an oriented smooth 4-manifold with a metric $g$ and a spin$^c$ structure $\fs$.
We can write down the SW equation
\[\left\{\begin{alignedat}{1}
F_A^+&=\sigma(\Phi)\\
D_A\Phi&=0
\end{alignedat}\right.\]
where $(A,\Phi)$ is unknown functions.
Here $A$ is a $\rU(1)$-connection on the determinant line bundle for $\fs$, and $\Phi$ is a positive spinor for $\fs$.
Roughly, $SW(X,\fs)$ is the signed count of
\[\frac{\#\{\text{solutions $(A,\Phi)$}\}}{\text{gauge symmetry}}.\]
It is typically an oriented compact 0-dimensional manifold.

$b^+\ge2$, the singularity with codimension 1?


\begin{lemma}
\[\Spin(4)\cong\Sp(1)\times\Sp(1),\qquad\Sp(1)=S(\H).\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$\Sp(1)\times\Sp(1)\to\SO(\H):(q_+,q_-)\mapsto(q\mapsto q_-q_+)$ is a double cover.
\end{proof}

Since $\Sp(1)\cong\SU(2):a+jb\mapsto\mat{a&b\\-\bar b&\bar a}$ canonically, we have four representations of
\[\Spin^c(4)=\frac{\SU(2)\times\SU(2)\times\rU(1)}{\Z/2\Z}\]
given by
\[\Delta_+:\Spin^c(4)\to\rU(2):[q_+,q_-,\lambda]\mapsto\lambda q_+,\]
\[\Delta_-:\Spin^c(4)\to\rU(2):[q_+,q_-,\lambda]\mapsto\lambda q_-,\]
\[\det:\Spin^c(4)\to\rU(1):[q_+,q_-,\lambda]\mapsto\lambda^2.\]
These representations induces positive spinor bundle $S^+$, negative spinor bundle $S^-$, and the determinant line bundle $L$.

We will frequently use the multiplication of quaternions for computations.

\section{Day 4: May 12}


Let $X^4$ be an oriented closed smooth 4-manifold together with a metric $g$ and a spin$^c$-structure $\fs$.
The \emph{configuration space} of the SW equation is
\[\cC(X,\fs,g):=\cA(L)\times\Gamma(S^+).\]
The \emph{gauge group} is
\[\cG(X):=C^\infty(X,\rU(1)).\]
Then, $\cG$ acts from right(?) on $A\in\cA(L)$, $\Phi\in\Gamma(S^\pm)$, $\omega\in i\Omega^+(X)$ such that
\[A\cdot u:=u^*A:=u^{-1}\,du+A,\qquad \Phi\cdot u:=\Phi u,\qquad \omega\cdot u:=\omega.\]



Let $\eta\in i\Omega^+$.
The $\eta$-perturbed SW equations are
\[\left\{\begin{aligned}
F_A^+&=\sigma(\Phi)+\eta,\\\qquad D_A\Phi&=0.
\end{aligned}\right.\]
It is introduced to get a degree of freedom for transversality.

\begin{lemma}
Let $\eta\in i\Omega^+$.
Then, $\mathrm{SW}_\eta:\cC\to i\Omega^+\oplus\Gamma(S^-):(A,\Phi)\mapsto(F_A^+-\sigma(\Phi)-\eta,D_A\Phi)$ is $\cG$-equivariant.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $u\in\cG$.

First,
\[F_{u^*A}^+=F_{u^{-1}\,du+A}^+=p^+d(u^{-1}\,du +A)=p^+d(u^{-1}\,du)+F_A^+.\]
Here, lifting $u:U\subset X\to\rU(1)$ to get $\tilde u:U\subset X\to\R$ locally in the sense that $u=e^{2\pi i\tilde u}$, it is locally exact, so it is globally closed
\[d(u^{-1}\,du)=d((2\pi i)^{-1}\,d\log u)=0,\]
and $F_{u^*A}^+=F_A^+$.

Second, recall that $\sigma(\Phi)$ is defined by a $\Spin^c(4)$-equivariant map $\sigma:\H_{S^+}\to\Im\H_{\Lambda^+}:\Phi\mapsto\Phi i\bar\Phi$, so it is clear.

Third, recall that $\nabla_A:\Gamma(S^+)\to\Gamma(T^*X\otimes S^+)$ is $\cG$-equivariant by $\nabla_{u^*A}(\Phi u)=(\nabla_A\Phi)\cdot u$.
On the Clifford multiplication, it is $c:T^*X\otimes S^+\to S^-$ induced from the quaternion multiplication $\H_{T^*X}\otimes\H_{S^+}\to\H_{S^-}$, so it is $\cG$-equivariant.
So we are done.
\end{proof}

By the lemma, the solution space $\mathrm{SW}_\eta^{-1}(0)$ has a right $\cG$-action.
We define the \emph{moduli space of solutions to the ($\eta$-perturbed) SW equations} is
\[\cM(X,\fs,g,\eta):=\mathrm{SW}_\eta^{-1}(0)/\cG.\]
It may not be good to call this the ``moduli space'', because it has no relation with stable bundles, representable functors, etc.

\begin{lemma}
If $b^+>0$, for ``generic'' $(g,\eta)$, $\mathrm{SW}_\eta^{-1}(0)$ is a (infinite-dimensional) submanifold of $\cC=\cA(L)\times\Gamma(S^+)\leftrightarrow i\Omega^1(X)\oplus\Gamma(S^+)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}[Sketch]
For the space $\mathrm{Met}(X)$ of all metrics on $X$, on the space $\bigcup_{g\in\mathrm{Met}(X)}\Omega_g^+$, we can consider an affine bundle $\bigcup_{g\in\mathrm{Met}(X)}(\cC_g\times\Omega_g^+)$.
Then, we can prove
\[\bigcup_{g\in\mathrm{Met}(X)}\bigcup_{\eta\in i\Omega_g^+}\mathrm{SW}_\eta^{-1}(0)\subset\bigcup_{g\in\mathrm{Met}(X)}(\cC_g\times\Omega_g^+)\]
is a submanifold.

Then, we finally get the desired consequence by the implicit function theorem and the Sard-Smale theorm for infinite-dimensional manifolds.
\end{proof}



\begin{theorem}
If $b^+>0$, for ``generic'' $(g,\eta)$, $\cM$ is a smooth \textbf{closed} manifold of dimension
\[d(\fs):=\frac14(c_1(\fs)^2-2\chi(X)-3\sigma(X))\in\Z,\]
where $c_1(\fs):=c_1(L)$, $c_1(\fs)^2=\<c_1(\fs)^2,[X]\>\in\Z$.
It is a consequence of the Atiyah-Singer index theorem and the Hausdorffness of $\cG$(?).
\end{theorem}

The number $d(\fs)$ is called the \emph{formal dimension}, and it maybe negative, and in this case $\cM=\varnothing$.
The compactness of $\cM$ is the most important property of SW theory.
An orientation, also called the homology orientation, on $H^1(x,\R)\oplus H^+(X)$ gives rise to an orientation of $\cM$.
Define
\[\mathrm{SW}(X,\fs):=\begin{cases}
\#\cM&\text{ if }d(\fs)=0,\\
\<\mu^d,[\cM]\>&\text{ if }d(\fs)\in2\Z_{\ge0},\\
0&\text{ if }d(\fs)\text{ is odd or negative,}
\end{cases}\]
where $\mu\in H^2(\cB,\Z)$ is defined as follows:
We define $\cB^*:=\cC^*/\cG\supset\cM$.
Pick $x_0\in X$ and let $\cG_0:=\{u\in\cG:u(x_0)=1\}$.
Since $\rU(1)$ freely acts on $\tilde\cB^*:=\cC^*/\cG_0$, we can define $\mu:=c_1(\tilde\cB\to\cB)$.


\begin{theorem}
If $b^+\ge2$, $\mathrm{SW}(X,\fs)$ is independent of the choice of generic $(g,\eta)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
If $b^+=1$, we get a pair $(\mathrm{SW}^+(X,\fs),\mathrm{SW}^-(X,\fs))\in\Z^2$ as an invariant of $(X,\fs)$.
For $b_1=0$, \emph{wall-crossing formula} $\mathrm{SW}^+(X,\fs)=\mathrm{SW}^-(X,\fs)\pm1$
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Simple type conjecture]
If $b^+\ge2$, then $\mathrm{SW}(X,\fs)=0$ if $d(\fs)>0$.
\end{remark}

So $d(\fs)=0$ is important for computations.



\subsection{How to think of the formal dimension}

An equivariant map $\mathrm{SW}_\eta:\cC\to i\Omega^+\oplus\Gamma(S^-)=:V$ induces a section of $s:\cC\times_\cG V=:\cE\to\cB:=\cC/\cG$.
Then, $\cM:=s^{-1}(0)$, and intuitively $d(\fs)=\dim\cB-\dim V$.
It is rigorously given by the Fredholm index of $ds:T_b\cB\to V$ at $b\in\cM\subset\cB$, and computed by the Atiyah-Singer index.



\begin{lemma}
Let $(A,\Phi)\in\cC$.
Then, the stabilizer of the action by $\cG$ on $\cC$ at $(A,\Phi)$ is
\[\mathrm{Stab}_{(A,\Phi)}=\begin{cases}
\{1\}&\text{ if }\Phi\ne0,\\
\rU(1)&\text{ if }\Phi\ne0.
\end{cases}\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If $\Phi\ne0$, for $u\in\cG$, the stabilization means $du=0$ with $\Phi(x_0)u(x_0)=\Phi(x_0)$ for some $x_0$ with $\Phi(x_0)\ne0$.

If $\Phi=0$, then $(A,0)u=(A,0)$ implies $u=1$.
\end{proof}
Therefore, $\cG$ acts from right on $\cC^*:=\cC\setminus(\cA(L)\times\{0\})$.

$X^4$ is an oriented

\begin{definition*}
$(A,\Phi)\in\cC$ is called \emph{irreducible} if $\Phi\ne0$ in $\Gamma(S^+)$.
\end{definition*}

It comes from the relation to algebraic geometry and the decomposability of vector bundles in Donaldson theory.

We want to know when the perturbed SW equations have a reducible solution.
If we let the space of perturbations $\Pi(X):=\bigcup_{g\in\mathrm{Met}(X)}i\Omega_g^+$, then $\Pi(X)\to\mathrm{Met}(X)$ forms a Hilbert bundle.
For $g\in\mathrm{Met}(X)$,
\[\cW_g(X):=F_{A_0}^++\Im(d^+:i\Omega^1\to i\Omega_g^+)\subset\Pi_g(X).\]
Then, $\cW_g(X)$ does not depend on the choice of $A_0$ because $A_0,A_1\in\cA(L)$ has $F_{A_1}^+-F_{A_0}^+=d^+(A_1-A_0)\in\Im d^+$.
Now we let $\cW(X):=\bigcup_{g\in\mathrm{Met}(X)}\cW_g(X)$.

\begin{lemma*}
For $\eta\in i\Omega_g^+$, the $\eta$-perturbed SW equations have a reducible solution iff $\eta\in\cW_g(X)$.
\end{lemma*}
\begin{proof}
Suppose there is a reducible solution.
It means that $\Phi=0$, so $F_A^+=\eta$.

Conversely, if $\eta\in\cW_g(X)$, then there is $a\in i\Omega^1$ such that $\eta=F_{A_0}^++d^+a=F_{A_0+a}^+$.
Thus we have a reducible solution.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition*}
The complement $\Pi(X)\setminus\cW(X)$ is homotopy equivalent to $S^{b^+(X)-1}$.
\end{proposition*}
\begin{proof}
$\cW_g(X)=F_{A_0}^++\im d^+$ is an affine subspace of $i\Omega^+$ of codimension $\dim\cH^+=b^+$ by the Hodge theory.
Then, since $\mathrm{Met}(X)$ is contractible, we are done.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark*}[Hodge theory]
In general, let $(X,g)$ be an oriented closed Riemannian manifold.
For $d^*:=(-1)^k*^{-1}d*:\Omega^k\to\Omega^{k-1}$, it is easy to show $(d\omega,\eta)_{L^2}=(\omega,d^*\eta)_{L^2}$, where $\omega\in\Omega^k$, $\eta\in\Omega^{k-1}$, and $(\alpha,\beta):=\int \alpha\wedge *\beta$ for $\alpha,\beta\in\Omega^k$.
The Laplacian is defined by $dd^*+d^*d$ which acts on $\Omega^k$.
Let $\cH^k(X):=\ker\Delta\subset\Omega^k$.
The Hodge theorem states that
\[\Omega^k=\cH^k\oplus\im(d:\Omega^{k-1}\to\Omega^k)\oplus\im(d^*:\Omega^{k+1}\to\Omega^k).\]
For $\dim X=4$, set $\cH^+:=\cH^2\cap\Omega^+$, called harmonic self-dual 2-forms.

We prove that $\dim\cH^+(X)=b^+(X)$, $\dim\cH^-(X)=b^-(X)$.
Before the proof, recall that there is also a canonical isomorphism $\cH^k\to H_{\mathrm{dR}}^k$ once fixing a metric.
Since $Q_X|{\cH^\pm(X)}$ is positively/negatively definite, $\cH^+(X)$ gives a model of $H^+(X)$.
Now give the proof.
For $\omega\in\cH^+(X)$,
\[Q_X([\omega],[\omega])=\int_X\omega\wedge\omega=\int_X\omega\wedge*\omega=\|\omega\|_{L^2}^2\ge0.\]
Similarly, $\omega\in\cH^-(X)$ has
\[Q_X([\omega],[\omega])\int_X\omega\wedge\omega=-\int_X\omega\wedge*\omega=-\|\omega\|_{L^2}^2\le0.\]
So we are done.

We also prove that $\Omega^+=\cH^+\oplus\im(d^+:\Omega^1\to\Omega^+)$.
If $h\in\cH^*$, then $*h\in\cH^{\dim X-*}$.
In the decomposition $\Omega^2=\cH^2\oplus\im d\oplus\im d^*$, if $\omega=h+d\alpha+d^*\beta$, then $*\omega=*h+*d^*\beta+*d\alpha$, so $h=*h$.
Thus, $\omega\in\Omega^+$ has
\[\omega=h+d\alpha+d^*\beta=h+d\alpha+*d\alpha=h+d^+(2\alpha)\in\cH^+\im d^+.\]
Further, $d^+(2\alpha)=d\alpha+d^*\beta\in\im d\oplus\im d^*$, so $\Omega^+=\cH\oplus\im d^+$.
\end{remark*}

\subsection*{Analytic preliminaries}

If $E\cong F$ and $\sigma(D):T^*X_0\to\Hom(E,F)_0$, then we call $D$ an \emph{elliptic operator}.

surjective at every point, then the implicit function theorem says that $\operatorname{SW}^{-1}(0)$ is a Hilbert manifold.

The gauge group $\cG_{H^{k+1}}:=H^{k+1}(X,\C)\cap C^0(X,\rU(1))$ is a Hilbert Lie group.
It acts on the domain and the codomain of the Seiberg-Witten map smoothly.
Then, $\cM:=\operatorname{SW}^{-1}(0)/\cG_{H^{k+1}}$ is a smooth manifold.
Following is the proof idea.

$\cG_{H^{k+1}}$-action has local slices on $\cC_{H^k}$, i.e. for every $\gamma\in\cC_{H^k}^*$, there is a submanifold $S\subset\cC_{H^k}^*$ containing $\gamma$ such that $\cG_{H^{k+1}}\times S\to\cC_{H^k}^*$ gives a diffeomorphism onto a neighborhood of the orbit $\cG_{H^{k+1}}$.
This slice $S$ gives a local chart of $\cB_{H^k}^*:=\cC_{H^k}^*/\cG_{H^{k+1}}$, making $\cB_{H^k}^*$ a Hilbert manifold.
$\operatorname{SW}^{-1}(0)\cap S$ gives a local slice for the action of $\cG$ on $\operatorname{SW}^{-1}(0)$, giving a local chart for $\cM$ near $[\gamma]$.

In fact, Hausdorff is not trivial.
We need to show $\cB_{H^k}^*$ is Hausdorff.
This follows from the compactness of $\rU(1)$.


The dimension of $\cM$ is computed by the middle cohomology of
